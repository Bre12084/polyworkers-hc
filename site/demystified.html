Chapter 1 · How We Got Here

America tied health insurance to the 9-to-5.

Then the way we work changed.

  1. 1940s
    Wartime wage limits

    With pay frozen during World War II, employers competed for workers by offering health insurance instead.

  2. 1954
    The tax break

    Federal tax law made job-based health benefits tax-free for workers. Buying your own plan got no such break.

  3. 2010
    The Affordable Care Act

    Created the Marketplace, so people without job-based coverage could buy a plan and get help paying for it.

  4. 2014
    Marketplaces open

    Tax credits help people earning up to 400% of the federal poverty level. Above that line, no help.

  5. 2021
    Extra help arrives

    The American Rescue Plan boosted the tax credits and removed the 400% cutoff. In 2022 Congress extended them through 2025.

  6. Dec 2025
    Extra help expires

    Congress didn't renew it. The cutoff came back on Jan 1, 2026.

  7. Jan 2026
    House votes to restore it

    The House passed a three-year extension. The Senate has not.

  8. 2027
    Prices rise again

    Benchmark Marketplace premiums are up another 13% on average.

Chapter 2 · Key Terms

Healthcare is confusing.

Here are a handful of key terms to understand the basics.

Premium
What you pay every month to have insurance, whether you see a doctor or not.
Deductible
What you pay for care each year before your plan starts sharing the cost.
Coinsurance
Your share of the bill after the deductible, often 20%.
Out-of-pocket max
The most you'll pay for covered care in a year. After that, the plan pays 100%.
Marketplace
The government-run site where you buy your own plan: HealthCare.gov or your state's version.
Premium tax credit
Federal help that lowers your monthly premium, based on your expected income.
Open enrollment
The yearly window to buy or change a Marketplace plan, usually Nov 1 to Jan 15.
HSA
Health Savings Account. Money you set aside before taxes for medical bills. Requires an eligible plan.

A cheap premium can hide a big deductible. Always compare what you'd pay in a bad year, not just each month.

Chapter 3 · Losing Your Employer Benefits

When you become your own boss, you are responsible for all of your benefits.

At a traditional job, your employer pays for a significant portion of your health insurance. When you work for yourself, that contribution disappears.

And there are other benefits you lose, too.

  • Unemployment insurance

    Lose a client? Unlike traditional employees, independent workers generally can't collect unemployment benefits.

  • Paid leave and disability

    If you can't work because you're sick or need time off, you may lose income unless you've arranged your own coverage.

  • Employer 401(k) contributions

    You can still save for retirement, but there's no employer matching your contributions. That money has to come from you.

Chapter 4 · What Changed in 2026

Updates to the ACA.

The Affordable Care Act is still here. But at the end of 2025, Congress allowed additional federal subsidies that helped millions of Americans afford health insurance to expire.

The result? Many people faced higher monthly premiums in 2026. Some switched to plans with bigger deductibles. Others dropped their coverage. With insurers raising prices again for 2027, the squeeze isn't over.

Chapter 5 · The Cliff Explained

One good month shouldn't cost you your health insurance.

Here's the deal. If you buy your own insurance, the government gives you a discount on your monthly bill. It's called a premium tax credit, but think of it as a coupon that gets smaller as you earn more.

For a few years, that coupon shrank gradually. Make a little more, get a little less help. Reasonable!

Not anymore. In 2026, if you're single and earn more than $62,600, the coupon doesn't shrink. It disappears.

One dollar over the line and you pay full price.

Now picture being a freelancer. Your income doesn't show up in neat paychecks. You have a slow spring, then land a big client in December. Great news, right? Except that invoice can push you over the line and cost you thousands in lost help.

It's not that freelancers earn too much. It's that the rules were written for people with steady paychecks.

Everyone agrees there's a problem. Nobody agrees on the fix.

The fights come down to three questions: who gets help, who pays for it, and how big the change should be. Here's each idea in plain English, with the best argument on each side.
